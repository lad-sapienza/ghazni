<p style="text-align: left;"><span style="color: #34495e;"><strong>12th International Congress on the Archaeology of the Ancient Near East (ICAANE)</strong></span></p>
<p>8. Islamic archaeology: <em>Continuities and discontinuities between a deep past and modernity</em></p>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><em><span style="color: #000000;">Valentina Laviola and Martina Massullo&nbsp;</span></em></p>
<h2 style="text-align: center;"><strong>Ghazni Sacred Landscape.&nbsp;</strong></h2>
<h3 style="text-align: center;"><strong><em>Ziyārāt</em>, Shrines and Burials from the Ghaznavid Period to the Modern Age</strong></h3>
<p>&nbsp;</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">A crossroads of the Iranian, Central Asian and Indian regions, in the late 10th century Ghazni became the capital of the Ghaznavid empire (977-1186), whose court was renowned as one of the most refined of the Iranian world. Once its political role faded due to the Mongol conquest, the city turned into a pilgrimage destination as attested by the astonishing number of mausoleums, shrines and burials revered as <em>ziyārāt</em>. These funerary sites covered an extended area in Ghazni from the citadel to the suburbs. The habit of visiting tombs belonging to eminent personalities &ndash; sultans, emirs and ulamas &ndash; kept these sites alive through time perpetuating their anthropological, religious and historical value. Thanks to a diachronic approach to the data collected on the field by the Italian Archaeological Mission in Afghanistan and the historical sources, we are finally able to compile a list of the identified ziyārāt and a tentative chronology of the sites&rsquo; occupational phases, as well as to develop a geo-referenced map. Such results shed light on the worship continuity that converted Ghazni from a royal to a devotional city, shaping the sacred landscape of the site across centuries.</p>
